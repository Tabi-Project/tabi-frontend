## Introduction

In March 2026, Tabi Academy ran its first Free Business Consultancy. The offer was simple: we would select a small number of women-led businesses, give them direct access to expert consultancy at zero cost, and ask only for a testimonial in return. No pitch decks. No applications designed to impress. Just real businesses with real challenges — and us, committed to helping them move.

We received 26 applications from Nigeria and Ghana. We selected seven. What followed was one of the most instructive weeks Tabi Academy has had to date — not because everything went according to plan, but because of exactly how it didn't.

This is a transparent account of what happened, what the sessions revealed, and what we are taking forward into the next edition.

## The Seven Businesses

The selection was deliberate. We chose for diversity of challenge type, sector, and geography — not just for the strongest applications. We wanted to test the programme across as wide a range of business contexts as possible.

## Six Things We Found to Be Consistently True

These patterns emerged across businesses in different industries, at different stages, in different cities. They came up again and again. We are documenting them because we believe they are broadly true — not just for the businesses we consulted with, but for most women-led businesses at similar stages across Nigeria and Africa.

  ### 01 — The stated problem is rarely the real problem.

  Every business came in with a named challenge — marketing, sales conversion, operations, financial records. In almost every case, the root issue was something different underneath. A fashion brand with a "marketing problem" actually had an undefined brand identity. A B2B services company that couldn't convert leads had a pricing model friction issue, not a marketing one. The most valuable thing a first session can do is excavate past the surface problem to the actual constraint.

  ### 02 — Most small businesses are sitting on more data than they realise — and not using any of it.

  Every business that had been operating for more than a year had sales history, customer contacts, and feedback — scattered across notebooks, WhatsApp chats, and bank statements. None of it was structured. This is the single most consistent gap we found: small businesses are making decisions by feel when they have the raw material to make them by evidence. Building even a basic customer database — names, purchase dates, spend, how they found you — is transformational at this stage.

  ### 03 — Brand identity is almost always defined by the founder — not the customer.

  When we asked business owners how their customers perceived their brand, most had strong opinions but had never actually asked. When one fashion business did ask — sending a simple WhatsApp message to ten loyal customers — the responses were striking in their consistency. The customers had a clearer, more unified sense of the brand than the founder did. "Hot babes that do nine to five" is a marketing brief. "Corporate modest fashion" is not. Building brand identity from real customer perception rather than founder assumption produces far more actionable results.

  ### 04 — Immediate wins and long-term strategy must be worked in parallel.

  Small businesses often oscillate between chasing the next sale and building for the future. What we found is that the two must coexist. The businesses that left with the most clarity had both an immediate action for this week and a longer-term structure to build toward. Motivation comes from seeing something work quickly. Infrastructure is what makes it keep working. Neither alone is enough — and a consultant who only gives you long-term strategy while you are worried about this month's revenue has missed something important.

  ### 05 — The best community strategy is the one a founder can actually sustain.

  Community building was one of the most frequently raised ambitions across the businesses we spoke to — and one of the most frequently stalled. The pattern: founders choose ambitious models (weekly newsletters, regular events, active WhatsApp groups) and then abandon them because they are also running the business, making the product, and managing customer relationships alone. The shift we recommend is from "what is the best community model?" to "what is the most consistent one I can realistically maintain right now?" Permanence beats scale every time at this stage.

  ### 06 — Engagement rate is not the same as impact.

  Of seven businesses selected, two engaged fully. Four had at least one meaningful touchpoint. One had no contact at all. By a surface reading, this looks like a poor completion rate. A more accurate reading: even one session with a founder who is time-poor, running a business alone, and managing everything else in their life is valuable — if that session is designed to leave something lasting. Resources, frameworks, and structured tools outlast the session itself. That is the design principle we are building from.

  ## An Honest Assessment

  We designed this programme to be useful. On that measure, we believe it was — for the businesses that engaged. The question we are sitting with is not whether the sessions were valuable. They were. The question is: how do we design a programme that captures more of the value for more of the businesses, especially the ones who are most stretched for time?

  What changed between March and the next edition:

  - A structured pre-session brief that businesses complete before being onboarded
  - Assigned session slots rather than open self-scheduling
  - A maximum of five businesses per edition to protect quality
  - A simpler, lower-friction feedback mechanism
  - Dedicated scheduling support for businesses outside major urban centres


## What we are changing for Q2

  - A confirmation step post-selection. Selected applicants will confirm participation and acknowledge the session expectations before being onboarded.
 -  Assigned session slots. Rather than leaving booking entirely open, we will offer each business a specific slot and a reschedule option. Self-scheduling alone is not enough.
  - A maximum of five businesses per edition, down from seven, to protect the quality of engagement and the time available for each business.
  - A simpler, lower-friction feedback mechanism. Slido and email reply are too high-barrier for a founder at the end of a busy week.
  - Dedicated scheduling support for businesses outside major urban centres. Logistics are a real barrier — not an excuse.


## What This Programme Is For

Tabi Academy's Free Business Consultancy exists to give women-led businesses access to the kind of strategic thinking that is usually expensive, exclusive, or inaccessible — especially outside Lagos, especially for businesses at the growth stage, especially for founders who are building without a team.

The March 2026 edition taught us that even one well-designed session can shift how a founder sees their own business. It taught us that data is everywhere in these businesses and almost nowhere is it being used. It taught us that the women who apply to this programme are serious, motivated, and deserving of more than good intentions.

We are taking every lesson from this edition into the next. The Q2 edition of the Free Business Consultancy opens soon.
